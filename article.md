# The Airport Sell-Off: A Story of What's Coming to Canada

- Sun Wu Kong

---

Let me tell you a story.

It begins on a September morning in Toronto, in a glass tower where powerful people gather to talk about money. The Prime Minister stands before them and says: we will sell the keys to our airports. Not the land, he says. Not the buildings. Just the right to run them. For fifty years. Maybe ninety-nine. Private hands will hold the reins. And everyone claps.

But who benefits? This is the question we must always ask. Not what they say. Who benefits.

To understand what comes next, we must look to other lands. To Australia. To Britain. To Brazil. The story is always the same. The names change. The pattern does not.

---

## The First Year: The Promises

When the deal is signed, they will say beautiful things. They will speak of efficiency. Of modernization. Of world-class service. The newspapers will print photographs of smiling executives. The politicians will cut ribbons.

But read the fine print. The fine print is where they hide the truth.

In Australia, when Sydney's airport was sold, the new owners promised jobs would be safe. And they were—for a while. Then the protections expired. And forty percent of the workers were sent home. Forty percent. Nearly half. These were not abstract numbers. These were people. Baggage handlers. Cleaners. Security guards. People with families.

The Canadian Labour Congress has studied these matters. They found that private investors need fifteen to twenty percent more revenue than the current system. This is not greed. This is the logic of capital. If you invest money, you must extract more money. This is the rule. It cannot be broken. It can only be hidden.

So where does this extra money come from? It comes from you. It comes from the worker. It comes from the community.

---

## The Third Year: The Fees Begin

At first, the changes are small. A fee here. A charge there. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that makes headlines.

But look to Perth, Australia. After privatization, the money collected from airlines for each passenger rose by more than sixty percent in ten years. Sixty percent. This money did not disappear. It went somewhere. It went into the pockets of shareholders. And the airlines? They passed the cost to you. To the passenger. To the family trying to visit grandmother. To the student flying home for the holidays.

In Brazil, researchers studied what happened when airports were sold. They found that ticket prices on routes with privatized airports were three to three-and-a-half percent higher than on routes between public airports. Three percent does not sound like much. But for a working family, every dollar matters. And three percent is only the beginning.

Canada already has some of the most expensive air travel in the world. We rank one hundred and first out of one hundred and sixteen countries. This is not an accident. This is a choice. And privatization will make it worse.

---

## The Fifth Year: The Nickel and Dime

Now the real extraction begins.

When a thing is owned by the public, the goal is service. When a thing is owned by private investors, the goal is profit. This is not a moral judgment. This is a simple fact.

So what happens? They look for new ways to charge you.

In Britain, where airports were privatized long ago, this is what you find: to drop someone off at the airport, you pay up to twenty-four dollars. To use a baggage cart—free in Canada today—you pay. Five English airports collected seven hundred and fifty-one million pounds in parking fees in one year alone. Heathrow charges up to ninety-eight pounds per day for short-stay parking.

The Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives has warned about this. They say for-profit airports will charge higher fees for everything that is not on the ticket. Parking. Ground transport. Retail prices. Food prices. All of it. All of it subject to increase. All of it to boost profits.

The Prime Minister says these things have nothing to do with ticket prices. Perhaps. But they have everything to do with the cost of travel. And they have everything to do with who can afford to fly and who cannot.

---

## The Tenth Year: The Profits Flow Out

Now the airport is a machine for extracting money. And the money flows outward. To shareholders. To pension funds. To infrastructure investors. To Macquarie Bank of Australia, which promised returns of over thirteen percent. To the Canadian Labour Congress's estimate: fifteen to twenty percent more revenue needed.

Where does this money come from? From higher charges. From pressure on workers. From the loss of long-term public value.

The pattern is the same in Australia. In New Zealand. In Portugal. In Britain. In the United States. Higher charges. Pressure on workers. Loss of public value. Every time. Every country. Every airport.

And what of the public? What of the community that once owned this asset? They are now spectators. They watch. They pay. They have no say.

---

## The Fifteenth Year: The Question of Quality

Here is where the story becomes interesting. Because private operators must choose. Do they invest in things that make money? Or things that serve people?

The answer is obvious. They invest in things that make money.

Retail space. Parking structures. Duty-free shops. These generate revenue. Security lines? Baggage systems? Terminal maintenance? These cost money. They do not generate profit.

So what happens? The airport becomes a shopping mall with planes attached. The concourse is beautiful. The queues are long. The staff is stretched thin. The maintenance is deferred.

A study from the University of Alberta found that private equity ownership led to fewer cancellations and higher customer satisfaction. This is true. But the same study found that fees increased by about twenty dollars per passenger. Twenty dollars. For every passenger. Every time.

The Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives warns that private companies will rebuild terminals as overpriced malls and food courts. They warn that cutting staff is not only about comfort—it is about safety. Fewer cleaners. Fewer baggage handlers. Fewer security personnel. This is what efficiency means. This is what profit requires.

---

## The Twenty-Fifth Year: The Lock-In

And now we come to the end of the beginning.

The concession agreement lasts fifty years. Maybe ninety-nine. The public has lost control. The private operator holds the keys. And there is no way back.

The Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives explains it clearly. Under the current model, airport authorities are not-for-profit. They reinvest surpluses. They return five hundred and twenty-five million dollars annually to the federal government. They made no profit at all off the nearly four billion dollars they brought in. Expenses and revenues were basically identical.

A privatized airport authority would not balance revenues and expenditures. It would drive up revenues. It would drive down expenditures. It would create a profit. And it would pay that profit to shareholders.

This is the logic. This is the system. This is what they call efficiency.

---

## But Wait—Is There Another Way?

Let me be fair. There are those who say privatization can work. They say it needs two things: a strong regulator, and an end to the rent system that takes five hundred and twenty-five million dollars from airports every year.

This is a serious argument. The rent system is indeed a burden. It makes Canadian air travel more expensive. This is true.

But look at the evidence. Look at what happened in other countries. The regulators were not strong enough. The private interests were too powerful. The pattern repeated itself. Higher charges. Pressure on workers. Loss of public value.

The Prime Minister says Canada is late to this. He says we can learn from others' mistakes. This is a hopeful thought. But hope is not a plan. And the logic of capital does not change because we wish it would.

---

## The Question We Must Ask

So here is the question. Not whether privatization will increase costs. It will. Not whether it will pressure workers. It will. Not whether it will reduce public control. It will.

The question is: who benefits?

The answer is clear. The private investors benefit. The shareholders benefit. The executives benefit. The politicians who cut the deals benefit.

And who pays? The worker. The passenger. The family. The community. The public.

This is the story of privatization everywhere. It is not a story of efficiency. It is not a story of modernization. It is a story of extraction. Of transfer. Of taking what is public and making it private. Of taking what is shared and making it profitable.

The airports of Canada belong to the people of Canada. They were built with public money. They are sustained by public workers. They serve the public good.

And now they are being sold. Not with a bang. Not with a revolution. But quietly. In a glass tower. On a September morning. While everyone claps.
