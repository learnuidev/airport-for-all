# What Will Happen to Canada If Carney Privatizes Airports: A Year-by-Year Projection

**The verdict from international evidence: higher ticket prices, new hidden fees, job cuts, and profits flowing to private investors—while Canadians already pay some of the highest air travel costs in the world.**

On September 15, 2026, Prime Minister Mark Carney announced that his government will open Canada's four largest airports—Toronto Pearson, Vancouver International, Montreal-Trudeau, and Calgary International—to private investment through "long-term concessions" [^3]. Carney says the federal government will retain ownership of the land and assets, and that the tens of billions raised will be reinvested in infrastructure, including smaller regional airports.

But research from labour organizations, think tanks, and international experience tells a different story: **the costs will be borne by ordinary Canadians**.

---

## Year 1–2: The Deal is Signed, Protections Expire

**What happens:** The concession agreements are negotiated and signed. Initial promises of "investment" and "efficiency" dominate headlines. But the fine print matters—specifically, the duration of any job protection clauses.

**International precedent:** In Australia, after Sydney Airport was privatized, the new owners **cut 40 percent of the workforce** once post-sale job protections expired [^14]. The protections were temporary; the cuts were permanent.

**Canada projection:** If similar job protection windows are included in Canadian concession agreements, expect major staffing reductions at Pearson, Vancouver, Calgary, and Montreal within the first two years. The Canadian Labour Congress estimates private investors would need **15 to 20 percent more revenue** to provide competitive returns—and cutting labour costs is one of the fastest ways to achieve that [^14].

The Union of Canadian Transportation Employees (UCTE), which represents workers at Calgary and Vancouver airports, was not consulted on the plan. National President Barry Tchir stated: "We're not even part of the discussion" [^8]. He directly questioned how private investors would generate "15 to 20 per cent return on investment" without "either increasing costs to passengers, or decreasing staffing levels, or [decreasing] other community investments" [^8].

---

## Year 3–5: The First Fee Increases

**What happens:** The new operators begin "optimizing" revenue. The most immediate lever is aeronautical charges—the fees airports charge airlines for landing, gates, and passenger handling.

**International precedent:** At Perth Airport in Australia, **revenue collected from airlines per passenger rose by more than 60 percent over a decade** following privatization [^14]. A 2021 econometric study of Brazilian airport privatization found that airfares on routes with at least one privatized airport were **3–3.5 percent higher** than on routes between two publicly managed airports [^5]. The researchers noted that market dominance "enforces the privatization impact on airfares" [^12].

**Canada projection:** Airlines will pass these costs directly to passengers. The CCPA notes that Canada's large airports' biggest source of revenue is Airport Improvement Fees, which make up 37 per cent of their revenue—$30 to $40 per ticket [^1]. These are currently restricted to capital improvements. But the other two revenue categories—aeronautical and non-aeronautical—are where a private company "would look for new revenue" [^1]. Aeronautical charges are directly controlled by airport authorities and could be raised, with airlines simply increasing ticket prices to ensure passengers ultimately pay [^1].

Given that Canada already ranks 101st out of 116 countries for air travel affordability, with taxes and fees comprising 25–35 percent of ticket costs, even a 3–5 percent increase compounds an existing problem [^14].

---

## Year 5–10: The "Nickel-and-Dime" Era

**What happens:** Privatized operators, needing to grow profits beyond aeronautical charges, turn to non-aeronautical revenue—charging for things Canadians currently get for free.

**International precedent (United Kingdom):**

- **Drop-off fees:** At privatized British airports, dropping someone off can cost as much as **$24** (or £28 for 30 minutes at Stansted) [^14].
- **Parking:** Five English airports collected **£751 million** in parking fees in 2025 alone. Heathrow charges up to **£98 per day** for short-stay parking [^13].
- **Annual parking revenue:** Around **£2 million a day** is spent on parking at five major UK airports [^13].

The UK Parliament has confirmed that "the provision and charging of car parking at airports (including drop-off and pick-up charges) is a matter for the airport operator as a commercial business to manage and justify" [^6].

**Canada projection:** The CCPA explicitly warns that "for-profit airports will certainly also look at charging higher fees for everything that's not on the ticket, from parking to ground transport, to retail prices to food prices. All will be subject to increased prices or decreased quality to boost profits" [^1]. A private company "would look quickly at increasing rents and parking fees to generate profits. These increases would be directly passed on to anyone who buys food in airports or parks in their parking lots" [^1].

Carney pushed back on this concern, telling reporters that airport restaurants, stores, and their management "has nothing to do with the price of tickets" [^3]. But the CCPA notes that while these are "somewhat avoidable for passengers—get dropped off, bring a sandwich—higher commercial rents also makes airports less appealing for businesses to seek to operate in" [^1].

---

## Year 10–15: Profits Flow Out, Investment Flows In (Selectively)

**What happens:** The airport becomes a profitable asset generating consistent returns for its private owners—often foreign pension funds and infrastructure investors. Dividends are paid. Some capital improvements are made, particularly in revenue-generating areas like retail and parking.

**International precedent:** The CCPA warns that "private investors will look to recoup their investment, and then some. Major airport investment firms like Australia's Macquarie bank have promised returns of over 13 percent" [^1]. The Canadian Labour Congress estimates that private investors would need **15 to 20 percent more revenue** than the current non-profit model generates [^14].

The recurring pattern across countries—Australia, New Zealand, Portugal, the United Kingdom, and the United States—is "higher charges, pressure on workers, and the loss of long-term public value" [^14].

**Canada projection:** The "tens of billions" Carney promises to raise will be a one-time windfall. The annual profit extraction will be permanent. As the CCPA noted: "Any way you cut it, privatizing airports is a good deal for private buyers and a terrible deal for travellers and workers" [^1].

---

## Year 15–25: The Service Quality Question

**What happens:** Investment priorities diverge from passenger needs. Private operators favor capital spending that generates revenue (retail space, parking structures) over spending that doesn't (security lines, baggage systems, terminal maintenance).

**International precedent:** The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) has noted that "under price cap regulation there may be incentives to increase profits by reducing costs. In some cases such cost cutting may lead to a lower quality of service" [^4]. The ACCC developed quality of service monitoring specifically as "a means of providing an indication of whether such cost cutting might be occurring over time" [^11].

A 2023 University of Alberta study found privately operated airports had fewer cancellations and higher customer satisfaction—but also **increased fees by about $20 more per passenger** [^14].

**Canada projection:** The CCPA warns that "longer term, private companies would look to rebuild terminals as overpriced malls and food courts to generate higher revenue" [^1]. The risk is that "efficiency" means fewer staff, longer queues, and deferred maintenance—while the retail concourse gets a facelift.

The CCPA also notes that cutting staff and staff pay "isn't only about comfort in an airport, it can also be about traveller safety" [^1]. Fewer cleaners and baggage handlers would likely decrease the quality of airports, but it could certainly increase profits [^1].

---

## Year 25+: The Monopoly Locks In

**What happens:** The concession agreement, often spanning 50 to 99 years, creates a private monopoly with no viable exit. The public has lost the ability to influence airport governance through democratic means.

**International precedent:** The CCPA warns: "While governments will recoil from any suggestion that asset recycling constitutes privatization because the asset ultimately remains in public hands, private interests gain its operation and control for what are often **50 to 99 year-long concessions**. As the P3 experience has shown, privately controlled public assets rarely operate in the public interest" [^1].

Under the current Canadian model, airport authorities operate as not-for-profit organizations, with surpluses reinvested into airports. Airport rents also return roughly **$525 million annually** to the federal government [^1]. "These airport authorities made no profit at all off the $3.95 billion they brought in in 2022—expenses and revenues were basically identical when aggregated across all airport authorities" [^1].

"A privatized airport authority wouldn't balance revenues and expenditures, instead it would attempt to drive up revenues and/or drive down expenditures to create a profit that it could pay to shareholders" [^1].

**Canada projection:** Once locked in, reversing course becomes prohibitively expensive. The governance structure would shift from local boards accountable to communities to private operators accountable to shareholders.

---

## The Counterargument: What Could Go Right

The Globe and Mail editorial board argues that privatization could work **if** two conditions are met: a strong regulator to approve or reject fee hikes above inflation, and a clean break from the current lease rent system ($525 million annually), which the editorial calls "one of the main reasons air travel is so expensive in this country" [^14]. With proper guardrails, privatization could "spur economic development, tourism and trade" [^14].

Carney has framed the plan as "concessions, not privatization," arguing that federal ownership of land and assets provides a safeguard. During a Q&A after his speech, Carney said the government studied other countries' approaches: "We're getting the benefit of being late to this, if you will, because we've seen transactions that don't work well … and we're going to apply those lessons" [^3].

Lawyer Karen Hennessey of Gowling WLG notes that the government's expectations on service levels, passenger costs, and employee management would need to be built into any concession agreement: "This isn't going to be the situation where the concessionaire is allowed to just take over and run it the way they would run any other business" [^15]. Concession deals could take **six to nine months or longer** to negotiate [^15].

But the evidence from Australia, the UK, and Brazil suggests that **guardrails are rarely strong enough** once private capital is embedded. The recurring pattern across countries and decades is consistent: higher charges, pressure on workers, and the loss of long-term public value [^14].
