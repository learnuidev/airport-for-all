# Public Runways, Private Profits: Who Pays When Canada Sells Its Airports

**Ottawa calls it efficiency. In Australia, Britain, New Zealand and Portugal, the evidence is already in: when a public airport becomes a private asset, the return has to come out of someone. This is the account of who.**

At 4:40 in the morning, the terminal at Montréal–Trudeau is already awake. Cleaning crews work the concourse before the first wave of departures. Ramp crews move baggage in the dark. Somewhere below, firefighters wait on a shift that the union says is too thin to meet international safety standards. By the time a passenger buys a coffee and complains that it cost too much, thousands of people have already done a day's work to put them in the air.

These are the workers the Canadian Labour Congress counted in its report, *Public Runways, Private Profits*: **435,800 people across Canada's airports, earning $32.9 billion in wages a year, producing $123.5 billion of economic output** [^16]. They are not a stakeholder group to be consulted. They are the infrastructure. Everything that moves at an airport moves because somebody lifts it.

So when, on 15 September 2026, Prime Minister Mark Carney announced that his government would open Canada's four largest airports—Toronto Pearson, Vancouver International, Montréal-Trudeau and Calgary International—to private investment through "long-term concessions" [^3], the first question worth asking was not whether the deal was a good one. It was who would be standing under it when it landed.

---

## A deal five years in the making, announced in an afternoon

The announcement itself was an afternoon's work. The preparation was not.

Budget 2025 had already said, in its "Supercharging Growth" section, that the government "will also consider options for the privatization of airports." The 2026 Spring Economic Update under the title *Strengthening the Future of Canada's Airports* repeated the intention, set up a $5 billion Trade Diversification Corridors Fund, and announced legislation to gather the information needed to evaluate reform options. Bill C-30, which received royal assent on 18 June 2026, amended the Canada Transportation Act to allow Ottawa to formally consider "options for the privatization of airports" [^16].

That makes this the most serious attempt at airport privatization in Canada since the National Airport System was built in 1994. Two earlier efforts died: Kim Campbell's government announced the idea in 1993 and lost the election weeks later; Justin Trudeau's government floated it in 2017 and shelved it after the pushback [^16]. This attempt is built differently—quieter, further advanced, and timed to a summit of global fund managers. The National Airport Policy of 1994 is not legislation but a governance programme of Transport Canada, which is why a sale may need remarkably little law-making to complete.

What was promised, in exchange, was money: the federal government would keep the land and the assets, and the tens of billions raised would be reinvested in infrastructure, including smaller regional airports.

---

## What is actually being sold

To judge that promise, you have to know what the thing is worth—and it turns out the public already has a balance sheet for it.

Canada's National Airport System holds 23 airports owned by Transport Canada and operated by non-profit airport authorities, whose locally constituted boards must by design include chambers of commerce, boards of trade, consumer groups, labour organisations and various levels of government [^16]. Ordinary people, in other words, have a seat at the table. Those authorities pay **$525 million a year in land rents—$7.3 billion cumulatively since the model began** [^16]. In 2022 the authorities took in $3.95 billion and made no profit at all: expenses and revenues were effectively identical, and whatever surplus exists goes back into the airport [^1].

So this is not a failing public asset being rescued by private money. It is a public asset that pays rent to the public, is governed with the public in the room, and publishes the statements that let anyone check the arithmetic. The president of the Canadian Airports Council, Monette Pasher, puts the awkward part plainly: "Canada's airports are already privatized" [^16]. She says it not to sell the idea but to warn against it. If a commercial, non-profit authority already runs the place at market discipline, what exactly does an owner who must return a profit add?

The answer comes from the people who would *be* that owner. Australian investment firms holding airports have promised their own shareholders returns above 13 percent [^1]. At the Senate Finance Committee, David Macdonald of the Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives did the arithmetic in public: a private investor would need Canadian airports to generate **15 to 20 percent more revenue than they do today**, and that gap "will come from somewhere"—airline charges, ticket prices, parking, retail, improvement fees, or reduced government rent. Parking and retail, he added, could not realistically carry a gap that size [^16].

Somebody pays. That is the whole argument in one sentence. What the report does is show, case by case, who that somebody has turned out to be.

---

## The fare you already find too expensive

Before following the money, it is worth starting where every traveller starts: at the price of the ticket.

Canada's user-pay model already ranks badly. The World Economic Forum puts the country 101st out of 116 on price competitiveness while ranking it 11th overall on travel and tourism [^16]. The International Air Transport Association found Canadian travellers paying over $70 more than comparable American round trips, with Airport Improvement Fees alone running more than $60 higher [^16]. Taxes and fees make up 25 to 35 percent of a Canadian ticket [^14].

Now follow the fee to its source. On a departing flight it is **$41.81 at Toronto Pearson, $45.99 at Montréal-Trudeau and $26.25 at Vancouver** [^16]. Those three airports took in $2.08 billion, $961 million and $717 million respectively in 2025 [^16]. Fees are not a rounding error here; they are a third of the business, and they are the one lever a private owner would most want to move. Today the Improvement Fee is ring-fenced for capital works. The CCPA warned that a for-profit operator "would look for new revenue" precisely where nothing is ring-fenced, and that aeronautical charges—landing, gates, handling—are directly controlled by the authority and simply passed on to the ticket [^1].

That is the theory. Australia is what happened when a country tried it.

---

## What happened in Sydney

In 2002, the price caps that had governed Australia's privatised airports were removed and replaced with a monitoring-only duty. The owners were now free to price as they wished.

They wished for more. From 2007–08 to 2017–18, **aeronautical revenue per passenger rose 61.5 percent at Perth Airport**, while aeronautical profit rose 50.6 percent at Melbourne and **133.4 percent at Perth** [^16]. Rod Sims, who chaired the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission, was later explicit that Australian consumers "bore higher costs for years" once owners had "free rein to extract profit wherever they saw fit" [^16]. A 2021 econometric study found the same effect in Brazil, where airfares on routes with at least one privatised airport ran 3–3.5 percent higher than between two publicly managed airports [^5].

Those are the numbers. The people are harder to see in them, so the report goes looking. Sydney Airport had signed a post-privatisation employment contract that preserved jobs and wages—for twelve months. It waited the year out, and then **cut 40 percent of the workforce** [^14]. In the same year it announced a **A$376 million profit** [^16]. The Community and Public Sector Union, which represented the workers who lost their jobs, drew the line between the two events and refused to separate them. Baggage handling, maintenance and ground support were then outsourced, and the union warned that security had been traded away along with the jobs [^16].

Canada has its own version of that story, and it has not required a sale. Under the present, unprivatised, non-profit model, airports are already contracting out services and engaging in "contract-flipping"—rotating contractors to wipe out seniority and wages. It has happened to ground handlers at Toronto Pearson and Montréal-Trudeau and to food service workers at Vancouver [^16]. Hundreds of union jobs have disappeared that way. The machinists and Unifor have both asked Ottawa to close the loophole by amending section 47.3 of the Canada Labour Code, and CUPE's Airline Division, representing 18,500 flight attendants, has warned that privatisation would deepen the pressure on wages and safety [^16].

Which is why the union at the airports now in scope was so blunt about the process. The Union of Canadian Transportation Employees, which represents workers at Calgary and Vancouver, was not consulted on the plan. Its national president, Barry Tchir, said: "We're not even part of the discussion" [^8]. Then he asked the question the whole debate turns on—how does an investor produce "15 to 20 per cent return on investment" without "either increasing costs to passengers, or decreasing staffing levels, or [decreasing] other community investments"? [^8]

Nobody has answered it. It cannot be answered.

---

## Then they come for the parking

Wages and landing fees are only the first drawer an owner opens. The one behind it is everything the passenger touches after clearing security.

British airports charge up to **$24 simply to drop someone off**, a service that remains free in Canada; baggage carts, also free here, can cost up to **$3.70** [^16]. Five English airports collected **£751 million in parking fees in 2025 alone**, with Heathrow charging up to **£98 a day** [^13]. Sydney's four-hour parking prices almost doubled after privatisation, train access costs rose, and a free drive-by pick-up area beside the terminal was closed [^16].

Carney has said that airport restaurants, stores and their management "nothing to do with the price of tickets" [^3]. The CCPA's reply was that such costs are "somewhat avoidable for passengers—get dropped off, bring a sandwich," but that higher commercial rents also make an airport less appealing to the businesses that operate in it [^1]. The sandwich is the point. It is the small change of a monopoly: individually defensible, collectively inescapable, and paid by people who have already bought a non-refundable ticket and have nowhere else to go.

And the charges are not the only thing that moves. Fifteen years in, the quality of the place is quietly renegotiated. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission described the mechanism with unusual candour: under price-cap regulation "there may be incentives to increase profits by reducing costs," and "in some cases such cost cutting may lead to a lower quality of service." The commission built service-quality monitoring specifically to detect it [^4] [^11]. The CCPA's warning is blunter: cutting staff and staff pay "isn't only about comfort in an airport, it can also be about traveller safety," and private companies would eventually "look to rebuild terminals as overpriced malls and food courts" [^1].

The counter-evidence deserves its place here too. A 2023 University of Alberta study found privately operated airports had **50 percent fewer cancellations** and higher customer satisfaction—and that they charged about **$20 more per passenger** [^14]. Both findings are real. The question is not whether a private owner can run a good airport. It is who collects the improvement, and who pays for it.

---

## Where the money went

Here the story turns, because the most revealing number in the whole file is not a fee. It is a sale price.

Margaret Thatcher's government sold the British Airports Authority—Heathrow and six others—in 1987, for what amounts to roughly **£3.42 billion in today's money** [^16]. Heathrow alone made **£917 million in profit before tax in 2024**, and paid £250 million in dividends to its ultimate shareholders [^16]. Four years of Heathrow's profit would exceed the entire inflation-adjusted sale price of all seven airports the public once owned.

Australia did the same thing and then watched the asset change hands again. Sydney Airport was sold in 2002 for **A$5.6 billion**—a price the government reached in part by approving steep charge increases before the sale, which made the asset more attractive to buyers. In 2022, the private owners sold it on to another consortium at an equity valuation of **A$23.6 billion** [^16]. More than double, for an asset the public had built and paid for.

Compare that with what the public gets now. The entire National Airport System returns $525 million a year in rent and has returned $7.3 billion since 1994 [^16]. A sale produces one payment. The extraction that follows is annual, indexed, and permanent. The value was created in public hands and captured in private ones—which is not a market outcome. It is a transfer, and the public is on the paying side of it.

---

## The accounts that would close

There is a reason this article can name the wages, the rents and the revenue streams of specific airports line by line, and the reason matters as much as the numbers do.

Toronto Pearson, Montréal-Trudeau and Vancouver all publish detailed financial statements, because they are non-profit authorities operating under a public governance mandate. Those statements show where every dollar comes from and where it goes. Aeronautical charges are 34 percent of revenue at Pearson, 28 percent at Vancouver, 38 percent at Trudeau [^16]. Salaries, wages and benefits are the largest controllable cost at all three: **$297 million at Pearson, $139 million at Vancouver, $108 million at Montréal-Trudeau** [^16]. Rent to Ottawa runs $236 million, $78 million and $107 million a year [^16].

Now look at those lines the way an owner looking for a return would look at them. There is the fare, which the passenger pays and the airline passes along. There are the wages, the fastest lever to pull and the easiest to explain in a boardroom. There is the improvement fee, which could be diverted, and the capital programme, where anything that does not generate revenue—the terminal that needs repair rather than the concourse that sells perfume—can be deferred. And there is the rent, which Ottawa could simply forgo in order to make the sale attractive to a buyer. On that last option, Macdonald was unforgiving: "That's just the federal government paying private investors. We could skip the whole charade of privatization and just pick some lucky company and give them a bunch of money from the federal government. We don't have to go through the charade of selling the airport" [^16].

A privately held airport owes the public none of this disclosure. The moment ownership changes, the documents that make it possible to write this article in the first place stop being published. The public would give up the asset and the ability to see what it earns, in the same transaction.

---

## Why none of this corrects itself

Everything above assumes that markets correct abuses. The word "efficiency" assumes it too. But an airport is not a market.

It is a natural monopoly. You cannot build a second Pearson next door, and you cannot walk to the one in the next province. When the price caps came off in Australia, the former competition commissioner watched owners maximise profits and raise fees, and watched consumers pay for years. In New Zealand the government rejected a recommended price cap in 2003, and two decades later the Commerce Commission itself found Auckland Airport on track for roughly **NZ$190–200 million in excess profit**, noting that travellers would bear it "while having no alternative air transport provider" [^16]. In Portugal, ten airports were privatised in 2012 and charges were set by formula rather than cost, running **30 percent higher than operations justified** until IATA and Airlines for Europe invoked ICAO's principle that charges should relate to costs and demanded the concession be renegotiated [^16].

Canada is more exposed to this than Europe, not less. Europe has rail: a traveller in Germany or France can refuse an airport's price and take a train instead. Canada's distances are continental, its alternatives sparse, its regional routes thin. After Australia's privatisation, regional air service visibly declined as the large airports raised charges beyond inflation; when both the airport and the airline are chasing profit, the least profitable routes go first [^16]. In a monopoly there is no competitor to force efficiency gains back to the public, so the gain stays wherever the owner decides to keep it.

---

## The other side of the argument, taken seriously

None of this means the case for the sale is empty, and it deserves to be stated at full strength rather than as a straw man.

The Globe and Mail's editorial board has argued that privatisation could work **if** two conditions hold: a strong regulator empowered to reject fee increases above inflation, and a clean break from the lease rent system, which the board calls "one of the main reasons air travel is so expensive in this country" [^14]. Karen Hennessey of Gowling WLG notes that service levels, passenger costs and employee management would all have to be written into the concession agreement, and that negotiation alone takes six to nine months or longer [^15]. Carney has framed the whole thing as "concessions, not privatization," and said of other countries: "We're getting the benefit of being late to this, if you will, because we've seen transactions that don't work well … and we're going to apply those lessons" [^3].

Both conditions are reasonable. Neither has ever survived contact with private capital. Australia wrote protections into the deal and removed the price caps five years later. New Zealand declined to regulate, and its own commission later found hundreds of millions in excess profit. Portugal wrote a formula and got charges 30 percent above cost. The protections are temporary; the cuts are permanent. And "applying the lessons" of transactions that did not work well requires someone to name what made them fail—which is a mandate to extract, in a market that cannot correct it.

---

## 4:40 in the morning, a few years from now

Return, then, to the terminal where this began, and to the question the announcement never answered.

The Canadian Labour Congress does not oppose the government's stated goals. Infrastructure investment, affordable air travel, a more competitive aviation sector: these are worth pursuing. What its evidence shows is that transferring ownership is not required to reach them, and would very likely prevent it [^16].

What it would require is less dramatic. Regulate contract-flipping properly, by amending section 47.3 of the Canada Labour Code, as the machinists and Unifor have asked. Fix airport firefighting staffing so that rescue crews can reach every runway within the three minutes ICAO requires, instead of waiting ten minutes or more for municipal help [^16]. And use the public balance sheet that is already collecting $525 million a year in rent to invest in the terminals and the regional routes that need it.

Do none of those things, and the freight is easy to picture. The first two workers in this article—the ones loading aircraft before sunrise—will be squeezed to manufacture a return, because wages are the largest controllable cost line and the quickest to cut. The third, the traveller paying $45.99 in Improvement Fees on a ticket that already feels too expensive, will pay for the rest. The land will still belong to the public. The profit will not.

A runway is a public thing. It was paid for by the public, it is worked by the public, and the money it earns currently comes back to the public in rents, in reinvestment, and in statements anyone can read. Handing over the right to charge for it is not a technical adjustment. It is a decision about who this country's infrastructure is for. Fifty-three percent of Canadians already oppose it [^16]—which suggests they have read the accounts more carefully than the people proposing to sell them.
